Succubus - Tales from the World, from Mythology, from the Bible

9/21/2026 — Berean Standard Bible, quoted in full

This study is about a pattern, not a slur on women as a sex. Scripture names a forbidden woman, an adulteress, and a spirit of seduction that pulls a man off the path of holiness. The modern world sells that pattern as freedom and branding. Mythology remembered it as a siren, a fox, a night visitor. The Bible names it without fur or fins, and also holds the man responsible: he walks to her house, and he dies for lack of discipline.

In Topics 666 this sits with seduction (201), adultery (188, 251), prostitution (202), and interacting with the spirit of seduction (645), under Sexual Corruption and Whoredom. The title word succubus is later folklore for a night lure that drains. It is not a King James or Berean Bible word. It is used here as a handle for the same snare across world, myth, and Scripture.

Writing is kept PG. Myths are folk warnings, not a second Bible. Scripture text: Berean Standard Bible (public domain; formerly called the Berean Study Bible). Every passage named below is quoted in full from the BSB.

I. TALES FROM THE WORLD

In first-world and second-world countries she is rarely called a harlot to her face. She is framed as freedom, branding, income, or "just chemistry." The pattern matches Proverbs 7: loud or visually loud, not staying in a covenant home, seeking the simple man, using flattery and appearance, treating stolen pleasure as sweet, and saying afterward that she has done nothing wrong.

First-world countries

United States, Western Europe, Canada, Australia, Japan, South Korea, and similar.

  • Content as a job. Subscription pages, "spicy" influencer feeds, music videos, and livestreams sell access to the body as a product. The pitch is entrepreneurship. The fruit is Proverbs 6:25: captivation by beauty and the eyes, without a covenant.
  • Apps as the street corner. Dating apps, "discreet" married-person apps, and private messages replace lurking at every corner. Twilight in Proverbs 7 is now midnight scrolling in his own house.
  • Images that never leave the house. What the man watches may be far away or staged. The snare is still in the heart. A man can be publicly "faithful" and privately captured.
  • Sugar and mentorship language. Arrangements sold as dinners, travel, and help with rent. Proverbs already named wealth leaving a man's house.
  • Openness as virtue. Multiple partners and unnamed bonds taught as honesty. Proverbs 30:20 is the old version: she eats, wipes her mouth, and says she has done nothing wrong.
  • The church and office version. Coworker, ministry partner, or friend who "understands him." Potiphar's wife is proximity, persistence, then harm if refused.
  • The Delilah pattern. Affection used to extract strength, secrets, money, or a calling. If you loved me, you would tell me / leave her / break your vow.
  • The Jezebel pattern in religious spaces. Teaching sexual immorality as enlightenment or "freedom in grace." Revelation 2 names that in the church, not only in a brothel.

Second-world countries

Much of Eastern Europe, Russia and neighbors, large parts of Latin America, Turkey, Mexico, and similar middle-income regions.

  • The same phone, a harder cash test. The branding is imported. Housing, visas, and remittances make the bargain more open. Nightlife and tourism companionship are often named as work.
  • Mistress culture. Being kept treated as status. The man's house is emptied toward another household (Proverbs 5:9-10).
  • Beauty as an exit from scarcity. Nightlife or marriage-for-papers advertised as a ladder. Not every woman in poverty is this type. This type uses flattery and the body as the ladder and treats covenant as optional.
  • Informal selling. Local chats and "VIP" groups replace corporate platforms in some states. She is still in the street and in the squares.
  • Religion mixed with the sale. Religious talk beside the hunt. Proverbs 7 already has vows in her mouth while she seeks the young man.
  • Trafficking beside "choice." Scripture still calls the snare a snare. The man who buys is not innocent.

What both worlds share: she is marketed as the prize of a successful man; holiness is mocked as repression; the wife of his youth is framed as boring; stolen water is called sweet; the end is still ruin of flesh, ruin of name, and a heart turned from God.

II. TALES FROM MYTHOLOGY

These are not Scripture. They are how many nations remembered a lure that looks beautiful, speaks well, delays a man from his duty, and leaves him weaker, bound, or dead. Read them as folk memory of the same snare, not as rival goddesses to honor. Where old religions named spirits and idols, the New Testament says the nations sacrificed to demons, not to God.

1 Corinthians 10:19-21

19Am I suggesting, then, that food sacrificed to an idol is anything, or that an idol is anything? 20No, but the sacrifices of pagans are offered to demons, not to God. And I do not want you to be participants with demons. 21You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons too; you cannot partake in the table of the Lord and the table of demons too.

The tales below stay PG. The danger is entrapment: a call off the road, not a scene to repeat. Each named term has a short hear MP3. German, Greek, Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Hindi, Malay, Indonesian, Russian, and Spanish names are spoken in those languages so the vowels are not an English guess. English literary names (siren, Circe, succubus, mermaid, Morgan le Fay) are spoken in English. Tap or click hear; in the PDF the same word is a link to the clip.

Water and voice

  • Sirens hear (Greek). They do not chase with a sword. They sing. Sailors turn the helm toward the sound and wreck. The lure is attention and longing, not a fair fight.
  • Lorelei hear (German recording of Loreley), nixies hear (German Nixe), rusalki hear (Russian русалка), mermaids hear. A fair woman at the water who calls a man off the path, the boat, or the wedding road. He follows and does not come back.
  • Calypso hear (Greek Καλυψώ, the nymph, not the music) and Circe hear (Homer; English literary "SUR-see"). Not monsters. They keep Odysseus with comfort, charm, and delayed homecoming. The wound is years stolen from covenant and calling.

Night visitors and life-drain

  • Succubus hear (medieval European lore). A night spirit in female form said to visit men in sleep, leaving them exhausted and tied to a fantasy. The meaning of the tale: a drain, a bond, and a secret life that holiness cannot share. This is the title's folk name.
  • Leanan sídhe hear (Irish; spoken here as LAN-awn shee). A muse-lover. She gives inspiration; the artist withers. Gift in one hand, lifespan in the other.
  • Lamia hear (Greek Λάμια) / empusa hear (Greek Έμπουσα). Beautiful at first look; the tale ends in the victim consumed. Folklore's way of saying the pretty invitation is not safe.

East and Southeast Asia

  • Fox spirits: huli jing hear (Chinese 狐狸精), kitsune hear (Japanese きつね), kumiho hear (Korean 구미호). A fox in a woman's shape. She marries, flatters, or serves in a house while remaining other than human. The man loses clarity, sometimes years, sometimes his life.
  • Jorōgumo hear (Japanese じょろうぐも). A spider in a woman's shape who binds. The picture is a web, not romance.
  • Pontianak hear (Malay) / kuntilanak hear (Indonesian). Often a vengeful spirit who appears as a woman on the road at night. The warning to men: do not follow the figure who should not be there.

South Asia

  • Apsaras hear (Hindi अप्सरा). Heavenly dancers in Hindu story sent so that a sage's austerity breaks. The point of the tale is distraction from a holy vow.
  • Yakshinis hear (Hindi यक्षिणी) in some later tales: beautiful guardians who can bless or ensnare. The danger-version is still a man pulled off his path by charm.

The Americas

  • La Tunda hear, La Patasola hear, and similar forest women (parts of Colombia and nearby). They appear as a loved woman or a beauty on a trail. The man who follows leaves the road and is lost. A later tell (one foot, a hollow back, a changed face) is the sign that he did not follow a wife.
  • La Sayona hear (Venezuela). A woman who appears to unfaithful men. Folk justice story: the snare comes dressed as desire and ends as terror.

Named enchantresses

Delilah is Bible. Morgan le Fay hear and similar court ladies are legend. Arthurian story uses a woman of the court who binds a knight with love-magic or a lie. Same moral: strength given away in private.

What the myths keep repeating: she is seen or heard first, not known in covenant. She calls him off the road, the ship, the vow, the fast, or the home. She uses song, flattery, loneliness, or a copied face. The cost is strength, years, sanity, or life. The man is almost never dragged against a locked will. He turns.

That is why these tales sit between the modern market and the Bible. The first world sells the siren as content. The second world often sells her as nightlife and a ticket out. The old world painted her as fox, water-woman, or night visitor. Proverbs names her as the forbidden woman with seductive words, whose house is the road to Sheol.

III. TALES FROM THE BIBLE

The longest portrait is in Proverbs. Then come houses and palaces: Potiphar's wife, Delilah, Solomon's wives, Jezebel. Then the man's own eyes. Then a city and a church dressed in the same spirit.

V. CLOSE

The world sells the snare as content and as a ladder. Mythology painted it as siren, fox, water-woman, and night visitor. The Bible names the forbidden woman, the adulteress, Delilah, Jezebel, and Babylon, and then tells the man to flee, to rejoice in the wife of his youth, and to be one spirit with the Lord. Charm is deceptive. Beauty is fleeting. A woman who fears the LORD is to be praised. The man who pleases God escapes the snare. The sinner is ensnared.
